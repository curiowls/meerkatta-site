Field notes

A place to begin again · November 19, 2026

Leave yourself a question.

An unfinished note can still tell you where to start next time.

Keep the point you reached.

A saved note says "Onboarding ideas." You recognize the project, but not the thought. Were you questioning the first screen, the setup steps, or how much explanation someone needs?

The topic isn't the stopping point. A title tells you what the note is about. One more sentence can preserve where your thinking stopped. That's the part worth leaving for yourself before moving on.

Leave a question with enough context.

Keep the unresolved part. The second note doesn't pretend the design is solved. It keeps the concern, the question, and something concrete to look at. You can return without first reconstructing the whole conversation you were having with yourself.

Make the next move small. "Finish onboarding" asks for an outcome. "Look at those three explanations" gives you somewhere to begin. You may discover that the original question was wrong. It still gives you something to respond to.

A return point, not another assignment.

A research connection

Planning the return

In four studies reported in 2018, Sophie Leroy and Theresa Glomb found that anticipating a time-pressured return to interrupted work could leave attention stuck on it. Briefly planning the return reduced that effect and protected performance on the interrupting task.

The research concerns work interruptions, not voice-note apps. Leaving a question in a capture is our practical adaptation, not a tested MeerKatta outcome.

You supply the question. MeerKatta can clean and structure a spoken capture; it doesn't need to answer it for you. On Mac, you can also continue a saved capture. That continuation control gives you a way to add more, while the question gives you something to say.

For the next unfinished idea, try ending with: "What I'm still wondering is..." Add the detail that made you wonder. A useful ending doesn't have to be a conclusion.

Source & further reading

Leroy & Glomb, Tasks Interrupted, Organization Science 29(3), 380-397 (2018). Publisher abstract reviewed November 19, 2026; the full study methods were not assessed for this note.
